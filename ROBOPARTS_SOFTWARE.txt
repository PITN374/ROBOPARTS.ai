PROJECT DEFINITION

ROBOPARTS.ai is a proposed robotics component intelligence, identity, manufacturing and sourcing infrastructure platform. Its purpose is to connect robotic components with the products, manufacturers, suppliers, physical instances, assemblies, robot configurations, technical evidence and commercial workflows in which they participate.

The central proposition is that a robotics component should be more than an isolated catalog entry. It should be describable through structured technical information, traceable to supporting evidence, associated with relevant physical and commercial identities, evaluated against system requirements, and connected to the organizations capable of supplying or manufacturing it.

ROBOPARTS is intended to help transform fragmented robotics supply-chain information into a more structured, searchable, comparable and interoperable information environment.

The intended progression is:

Robotics requirements -> Component identification -> Technical description -> Manufacturer and supplier discovery -> Evidence and compatibility assessment -> Sourcing and RFQ workflows -> Assembly and robot configuration -> Lifecycle records and downstream integration

This document describes the project's intended software architecture and development direction. Proposed capabilities must not be interpreted as proof that every module, interface, integration or commercial service is operational.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Robotics developers often obtain component information from manufacturer catalogs, distributor listings, engineering drawings, supplier quotations, internal bills of materials, enterprise systems and maintenance records.

These sources can use different product names, part numbers, data formats, specifications and identifiers. A matching product description does not necessarily establish that two records represent the same revision, that a physical unit is authentic, or that a component is suitable for a particular robot.

The problem becomes more complex when components are installed, removed, replaced, repaired, transferred or modified.

ROBOPARTS is designed to preserve the distinctions among:

A component category.
A commercial product definition.
A particular physical component instance.
An assembly position or bill-of-materials relationship.
A robot or machine.
A time-specific robot configuration.
A manufacturer, distributor, supplier or buyer.
A technical claim and its supporting evidence.
A lifecycle event and the system that recorded it.

These distinctions support more reliable identity resolution, sourcing decisions, compatibility assessment and lifecycle reconstruction.

CORE SOFTWARE RESPONSIBILITIES

3.1 Component intelligence

Represent robotic components using structured, versioned information.

Potential component classes include:

Joint actuators, servo motors and BLDC motors.
Harmonic, planetary and cycloidal reducers.
Robotic hands, grippers and end effectors.
Sensors, cameras, LiDAR and encoders.
Motor controllers, embedded computers and communication modules.
Batteries, power systems, connectors and wiring harnesses.
Structural components, CNC-machined parts and mechanical assemblies.
Humanoid robot subsystems and other robotic hardware.

Component records may include manufacturer, model, revision, identifiers, dimensions, materials, performance attributes, interfaces, protocols, availability and supporting documentation.

Missing or uncertain information must remain explicitly identified rather than being invented.

3.2 Persistent identity

Maintain stable internal references for relevant entities and support multiple external identifiers where necessary.

The identity model should distinguish manufacturer part numbers, supplier SKUs, serial numbers, lot identifiers, internal material numbers and other source-system references.

Identity resolution should preserve original values, identify ambiguous matches and record the evidence supporting a proposed association.

Conflicting records must not be silently merged. A component replacement must preserve the historical identity and records of both the removed component and its replacement.

3.3 Manufacturer and supplier intelligence

Connect component requirements with relevant manufacturers, distributors, contract manufacturers and other suppliers.

Potential matching criteria include:

Component and manufacturing capabilities.
Materials and production processes.
Technical requirements and tolerances.
Quality systems and certifications, where verified.
Production capacity and minimum order quantities.
Geographic and logistics constraints.
Lead times and commercial conditions.
Evidence quality and source reliability.

Supplier recommendations should explain the criteria and evidence behind them. A match is not a guarantee of qualification, availability, compatibility or successful delivery.

3.4 Evidence and provenance

Associate material claims with source records and supporting evidence.

An evidence record may include:

Evidence identifier.
Source URL or document reference.
Source type and issuing organization.
Retrieval or recording timestamp.
Relevant excerpt or data location.
Claim or attribute supported.
Verification status and method.
Confidence information, where defined.
Applicable version and known limitations.

The system should distinguish manufacturer-declared information, independently measured results, inferred values, disputed claims and unverified information.

3.5 Component compatibility and capability assessment

Represent the technical properties needed to evaluate whether a component may satisfy a defined requirement.

A compatibility assessment should consider applicable interfaces, dimensions, operating ranges, power requirements, mechanical constraints, protocols, environmental limits, dependencies and verification status.

The software should report exact matches, potential matches, incompatibilities and unresolved requirements separately.

Automated matching is a decision-support function. It does not replace engineering review or system-specific validation where those are required.

======================================================================

RFQ AND COMMERCIAL SOURCING WORKFLOWS

ROBOPARTS may support structured requests for quotation (RFQs), supplier responses and commercial comparisons.

An RFQ can include:

Component identifier and description.
Drawing reference and revision.
Required quantity and unit of measure.
Technical specifications and dimensional tolerances.
Materials and manufacturing processes.
Inspection and quality requirements.
Required certifications, where applicable.
Prototype, tooling and production costs.
Volume pricing and minimum order quantities.
Lead times and delivery schedule.
Packaging, shipping and warranty terms.
Payment terms, quotation validity and submission deadline.

Supplier offers should be retained with their source, timestamp, scope and commercial conditions.

Comparisons should normalize relevant currencies, quantities, cost categories and delivery assumptions. Missing specifications, unverified claims, capacity concerns and unresolved technical differences should be flagged.

The system must not fabricate quotations, represent an unconfirmed supplier as qualified, or place orders without the required authorization.

======================================================================

ROBOT CONFIGURATION AND LIFECYCLE

A robot configuration should identify the relevant robot, configuration version, installed component instances, assembly positions, hardware revisions and applicable software or firmware references.

Lifecycle events may include manufacture, inspection, receipt, installation, calibration, maintenance, transfer, removal, replacement and decommissioning.

Each event should have a stable identifier, event type, subject, relevant relationships, source evidence, validation status and timestamps.

Where practical, distinguish:

Valid time: when the event or relationship applied in the physical or operational environment.
Recorded time: when the event or relationship was entered into the information system.

This distinction supports historical reconstruction when an event is recorded after it occurred.

A configuration change should not erase prior configuration records. Historical records should remain available according to the system's access, retention and privacy policies.

======================================================================

DIGITAL IDENTITY AND NAMESPACE CONCEPTS

ROBOPARTS explores persistent digital identity for physical components and their relationships to machines, organizations, transactions and lifecycle events.

The project's documented namespace concepts, including .Actuator, are intended to support organized references and discovery for robotics-related entities.

Namespace labels, domain names and internal identifiers must not automatically be treated as proof of global uniqueness, legal ownership, physical authenticity or registration status.

A production implementation must document identifier generation, resolution rules, collision handling, external identifier mappings and the evidence supporting identity claims.

======================================================================

DIGITAL TWINS AND INTEROPERABILITY

A digital representation may connect a robot's recorded identity, component instances, assembly relationships, configuration, capabilities, maintenance events and supporting evidence.

The intended uses include configuration management, simulation, diagnostics, sourcing, lifecycle management and integration with downstream robotics applications.

Potential integrations include ERP, PLM, MES, inventory, procurement, maintenance systems, robotics middleware and other authorized data providers.

Existing systems should remain authoritative for the records they own unless an explicit integration agreement defines another arrangement.

A digital record does not independently prove that the recorded component is physically installed or that the recorded configuration matches the actual machine.

PROPOSED SOFTWARE MODULES

A modular implementation may include:

Component Registry: products, component classes and physical instances.
Identity Resolution: identifier mapping, duplicate detection and conflict handling.
Organization Registry: manufacturers, suppliers, distributors and buyers.
Evidence and Provenance: source records, attribute claims and verification status.
Capability and Compatibility Engine: requirements, technical matching and limitations.
Supplier Discovery: permitted source searches and manufacturer classification.
RFQ Management: requirements, quotation collection and comparison.
Lifecycle Service: component events and historical reconstruction.
Configuration Service: robots, assemblies, revisions and installed components.
Digital Twin Adapter: exchange of selected configuration and lifecycle data.
API and Integration Layer: validated access to authorized records.
Audit and Reporting: operational results, changes, errors and evidence references.

These are proposed logical modules. Their existence and completion must be confirmed against actual source code, tests and deployment evidence.

======================================================================

DATA AND API DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Use stable identifiers, versioned schemas, explicit units and defined validation rules.

Separate product definitions, physical instances, assemblies, robots, evidence, organizations and lifecycle events.

Retain source-system identifiers and provenance. Support extensible component classifications without silently changing the meaning of existing fields.

Potential API resources include components, products, physical instances, organizations, suppliers, evidence, compatibility assessments, RFQs, quotations, robot configurations and lifecycle events.

Any implemented interface should define authentication, authorization, input validation, versioning, error behavior, rate limits and audit requirements.

Illustrative endpoints are not evidence of an existing API.

======================================================================

SECURITY, INTEGRITY AND GOVERNANCE

Use least-privilege access, secure secret management, input validation, appropriate data retention and audit logging.

Confidential supplier information, sensitive commercial records and personal information should not be published in public repositories or exposed through public APIs without authorization.

Optional cryptographic hashing or tamper-evident anchoring may help identify changes to selected records. Such mechanisms do not prove that a source claim is true, a physical component is authentic or an event occurred.

If blockchain anchoring is selected, document canonical serialization, hash coverage, version handling, anchor references, verification procedures and failure behavior. It should be justified by a demonstrable requirement rather than assumed to be mandatory.

======================================================================

DEVELOPMENT AND VALIDATION ROADMAP

Suggested implementation stages:

Stage 1: Review existing repositories, schemas, integrations and data; document the baseline and unresolved questions.

Stage 2: Implement a bounded component registry and evidence-linked data workflow.

Stage 3: Implement persistent identity, identifier resolution, component relationships and lifecycle history.

Stage 4: Add supplier discovery, classification, RFQ creation and quotation comparison.

Stage 5: Add configuration reconstruction, compatibility assessment and selected digital-twin integrations.

Stage 6: Evaluate integrity mechanisms, interoperability and production requirements.

Every stage should have documented acceptance criteria, reproducible tests, observed results, known defects and implementation evidence.

======================================================================

DEFINITION OF SUCCESS

A successful implementation should demonstrate, within an explicitly defined scope, that it can:

Create, validate, search and retrieve component records.
Distinguish products from individual physical instances.
Preserve multiple identifiers and report ambiguous matches.
Link material claims to traceable source evidence.
Record and reconstruct component lifecycle events.
Preserve historical robot configurations.
Compare components and supplier offers against stated requirements.
Generate RFQs from validated technical inputs.
Report missing information and unresolved compatibility questions.
Enforce authorization and protect confidential records.
Produce reproducible test results and accurate operational reports.

Completion must be demonstrated through source code, tests, sample data, documented interfaces and observed results—not through architectural descriptions alone.

RELATIONSHIP TO THE PITN.AI ECOSYSTEM

ROBOPARTS.ai is the component identity, technical information, manufacturing and sourcing layer of the intended PITN.ai ecosystem.

ROBOTRAITS.ai provides a complementary vocabulary for describing robotic capabilities, behavioral traits, profiles and constraints.

ROBOAGENTIC.ai is the proposed intelligent-agent layer that may use component and capability information to conduct authorized research, evaluate options, prepare RFQs and coordinate approved workflows.

PITN.ai provides the broader project identity and intended knowledge and infrastructure context.

These relationships describe an intended integration architecture. Each connection must be validated independently before being represented as operational.
