1. PURPOSE AND FRAMEWORK

ROBOPARTS establishes a standardized framework for identifying, describing, evaluating, matching, and tracking robotic actuators and their associated components.

Actuators are the mechanical power systems responsible for creating controlled movement in robots. They convert electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, or other forms of energy into physical motion.

In humanoid robots, actuator performance directly affects movement, strength, precision, efficiency, responsiveness, and overall mechanical capability.

These characteristics influence:

Torque and rotational force.
Speed and acceleration.
Joint responsiveness.
Mechanical efficiency.
Thermal performance.
Payload capacity.
Robot weight and balance.
Electrical energy consumption.
Manufacturing and maintenance costs.
Long-term reliability.

A humanoid robot may contain dozens of actuators distributed throughout its hips, knees, ankles, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hands, neck, and torso.

Each actuator contributes to the robot's overall performance. Differences in actuator design can affect the mechanical requirements, electrical architecture, thermal demands, and operating capabilities of the entire machine.

ROBOPARTS introduces a persistent identity and information structure that connects individual actuators to their components, manufacturers, specifications, applications, verification records, and replacement history.

The objective is to establish a consistent foundation for robotic hardware identification and engineering compatibility.

2. THE ACTUATOR DESIGN AND TORQUE DISTRIBUTION PRINCIPLE

Core engineering principle: The design of an actuator determines where the system carries the burden of producing torque.

Every actuator must generate useful mechanical force while operating within physical and economic limits involving size, weight, speed, efficiency, heat, precision, shock resistance, and manufacturing cost.

Two major design approaches illustrate how these requirements can be distributed differently.

HIGHER-REDUCTION ARCHITECTURE

This approach combines a relatively smaller, faster motor with a higher-ratio gearbox.

The gearbox increases output torque while reducing rotational speed.

Its design places greater importance on:

Precision transmission components.
Gearbox efficiency.
Mechanical tolerances.
Bearing performance.
Backlash management.
Transmission durability.
LOWER-REDUCTION / QUASI-DIRECT-DRIVE ARCHITECTURE

This approach combines a larger, higher-torque motor with a lower-ratio transmission.

More of the torque-generation responsibility remains with the motor rather than the gearbox.

Its design places greater importance on:

Motor electromagnetic performance.
Copper winding capacity.
Permanent magnet performance.
Electrical current delivery.
Motor cooling.
Thermal management.
Power electronics.

Neither architecture provides a universal solution for every robotic joint.

The appropriate design depends on joint loading, operating speed, movement requirements, available installation space, thermal conditions, manufacturing capabilities, and total system cost.

ROBOPARTS treats these architectural differences as identifiable engineering characteristics rather than interchangeable product descriptions.

3. ACTUATOR COMPONENT ARCHITECTURE

Controller.

Housing.

Electrical connectors.
Cable assemblies.

A joint module generally describes the mechanical assembly installed at a robotic joint.

A motor driver provides the power electronics used to operate and control the motor.

An actuator may represent an integrated assembly containing mechanical and electrical components or a more limited component package.

For consistent identification and procurement, ROBOPARTS distinguishes between the complete actuator and the individual components included in its assembly.

Product records identify all included and excluded items.

These records specifically distinguish the presence of a motor driver, encoder, brake, cabling, housing, cooling system, mounting hardware, software, and control firmware.

An actuator product description alone does not establish that these additional components are included.

This structure creates a consistent basis for product comparison, engineering evaluation, supplier quotations, and procurement.

7. GEARBOX AND TRANSMISSION ARCHITECTURES

ROBOPARTS identifies the transmission technology associated with each actuator.

The gearbox is a major contributor to output torque, rotational speed, mechanical efficiency, precision, and overall actuator behavior.

7.1 PLANETARY GEARBOX

A planetary gearbox uses a central sun gear to drive multiple planet gears positioned inside an outer ring gear.

Typical characteristics include:

High mechanical efficiency.
Established manufacturing processes.
Scalable production.
Multiple available reduction ratios.
Good load distribution.
Potential backlash.
Increased complexity in multi-stage configurations.
7.2 HARMONIC / STRAIN-WAVE GEARBOX

A harmonic gearbox uses a flexible transmission element to achieve high reduction ratios within a compact mechanical arrangement.

Typical characteristics include:

High reduction ratios.
Compact packaging.
Low backlash.
Precision positioning.
Specialized manufacturing requirements.
Flexible-element fatigue considerations.
7.3 CYCLOIDAL GEARBOX

A cycloidal gearbox uses eccentric motion and rolling contact between internal transmission components to produce mechanical reduction.

Typical characteristics include:

High load capacity.
Strong shock resistance.
Low backlash potential.
Mechanical durability.
Multiple load-bearing contact points.
Packaging and mass considerations.
7.4 QUASI-DIRECT DRIVE (QDD)

Quasi-direct drive represents an actuator design philosophy rather than a separate gearbox family.

It generally combines a high-torque motor with a relatively low-ratio transmission.

A conceptual reduction range commonly associated with QDD designs is approximately 3:1 to 10:1, although actual implementations vary.

QDD architectures place greater responsibility on the motor and electrical system to generate the required output torque.

Their design emphasizes responsiveness, backdrivability, force control, and reduced transmission complexity.

ROBOPARTS classifies QDD as an actuator architecture and separately identifies the actual gearbox technology used within the assembly.

B. ELECTRICAL AND CONTROL COMPATIBILITY

Evaluation attributes include:

C. PERFORMANCE COMPATIBILITY

Evaluation attributes include:

Continuous torque.
Peak torque.
Operating speed.
Torque density.
Thermal capability.
Duty cycle.
Backlash.
Shock tolerance.
COMPATIBILITY CLASSIFICATION

Every compatibility assessment uses one of five standardized results:

COMPATIBLE — The available engineering information demonstrates that the component meets the defined requirements.

CONDITIONALLY COMPATIBLE — The component can meet the requirements when specified modifications, additional components, or operating restrictions are applied.

INCOMPATIBLE — One or more critical requirements are outside the component's capabilities.

INSUFFICIENT DATA — Available information is not sufficient to complete the assessment.

NOT VERIFIED — Compatibility has not been established through the required engineering review or testing.

A dimensional match alone does not establish complete actuator compatibility.

Mechanical, electrical, control, performance, and thermal requirements must be considered together.

12. PERFORMANCE VERIFICATION AND DATA INTEGRITY

This distinction establishes a consistent foundation for evaluating technical claims.

12.2 MEASURED PERFORMANCE

Results obtained through a documented physical test or measurement process.

12.3 VERIFIED PERFORMANCE

Performance supported by an identified test procedure, documented results, and accepted verification criteria.

Original manufacturer specifications and performance claims remain associated with their source and revision.

Subsequent changes to a component or its specifications do not overwrite historical records.

This preserves engineering traceability and supports reliable product comparisons over time.

13. ACTUATOR ECONOMICS AND SUPPLY CHAIN

Actuator architecture influences where manufacturing costs, material requirements, and supplier dependencies are concentrated.

Higher-reduction architectures may place greater economic importance on:

Precision gears.
Specialized machining.
Tight manufacturing tolerances.
Transmission assembly.
Precision bearings.

Lower-reduction architectures may place greater economic importance on:

High-performance motors.
Copper windings.
Permanent magnets.
Electrical steel.
Power electronics.
Thermal management.
High-current electrical components.

ROBOPARTS connects actuator identities to their underlying manufacturers, component suppliers, and sourcing relationships.

This structure supports visibility into:

Supplier concentration.
Alternative manufacturers.
Material dependencies.
Component availability.
Product revision differences.
Replacement options.
Manufacturing dependencies.
Potential supply-chain risks.

The actuator's contribution to total humanoid bill-of-materials cost varies according to robot design, production volume, actuator architecture, component sourcing, and manufacturing strategy.

ROBOPARTS treats actuator economics as a measurable part of the complete robotic hardware supply chain rather than a fixed industry-wide percentage.

15. ADAPTIVE HARDWARE RELATIONSHIPS AND ROBOT CAPABILITY

Actuator replacement, modification, or upgrading can change the physical characteristics and operating capabilities of a robot.

ROBOPARTS maintains the relationship between:

Previous actuator identity.
Replacement actuator identity.
Robot identity.
Joint identity.
Installation event.
Configuration revision.
Performance test.
Verified capability.

Every hardware change becomes part of the robot's component history.

A replacement actuator may introduce changes in torque, speed, weight, electrical consumption, thermal requirements, and mechanical behavior.

These changes are recorded against the affected joint and robot configuration.

An actuator upgrade does not automatically establish an increase in verified robot capability.

Any resulting capability change remains subject to engineering evaluation, system-level testing, and appropriate authorization.

This approach maintains a traceable connection between the hardware installed in a robot and its documented performance.

16. ROBOPARTS LONG-TERM TECHNICAL FOUNDATION

The ROBOPARTS actuator framework establishes a common information structure connecting robotic components to their specifications, manufacturers, applications, compatibility records, and lifecycle history.

Its proposed architecture supports several interconnected capabilities:

Component Identity

Persistent identification of actuators and their independently identifiable subcomponents.

Engineering Standardization

Consistent organization of mechanical, electrical, thermal, and performance specifications.

Hardware Compatibility

Structured evaluation of components against actual robotic joint requirements.

Performance Verification

Separation of manufacturer claims, measured results, and verified engineering performance.

Supplier Intelligence

Connections between actuator assemblies, component manufacturers, materials, and supply-chain relationships.

Procurement Integration

Continuity between component discovery, engineering review, quotations, testing, and production sourcing.

Hardware Lifecycle Management

Traceable records of component installation, replacement, modification, and performance history.

The long-term objective is to create a connected hardware information framework that improves the identification, evaluation, sourcing, and management of robotic components.

ROBOPARTS positions actuator identity as a foundational element of a broader robotic hardware standard.
