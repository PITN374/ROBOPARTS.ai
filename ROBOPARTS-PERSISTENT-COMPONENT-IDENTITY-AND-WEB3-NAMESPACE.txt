PURPOSE

ROBOPARTS.ai establishes a structured framework for identifying, describing, connecting, organizing, verifying, and managing the physical components that make up robotic systems.

ROBOPARTS is a core physical-component intelligence layer within the broader PITN.ai robotics ecosystem.

Its primary purpose is to establish persistent digital identity and structured relationships around physical robotic components throughout their manufacturing, distribution, procurement, integration, deployment, maintenance, replacement, and lifecycle.

The underlying problem is that the same physical component may be represented differently across multiple independent information systems.

A manufacturer may use a Manufacturer Part Number (MPN).

A distributor may use a SKU.

An engineering department may use a CAD reference.

A manufacturer of a robot may use a BOM identifier.

An ERP system may assign a material number.

A service organization may use a maintenance identifier.

A deployed robot may contain an individually serialized physical component.

These references may describe the same product or physical object without maintaining a consistent connection between them.

PITN addresses this identity fragmentation through persistent component identity and relationship architecture.

ROBOPARTS applies this model specifically to robotics.

==================================================

CORE ARCHITECTURAL PRINCIPLE

The physical component is the foundation of the identity relationship.

The namespace provides an identifiable entry point.

Persistent component identity provides continuity.

Structured relationships connect the component to its associated information.

The conceptual relationship is:

PHYSICAL COMPONENT | v PERSISTENT DIGITAL IDENTITY | v NAMESPACE | v MANUFACTURER | v PRODUCT | v PART NUMBER / SKU | v REVISION / CONFIGURATION | v LOT / SERIAL NUMBER | v BOM POSITION | v ROBOT INSTALLATION | v PERFORMANCE / VERIFICATION | v SERVICE / REPLACEMENT | v LIFECYCLE HISTORY

The persistent identity is intended to connect existing information without requiring organizations to abandon their established ERP, PLM, MES, CAD, engineering, manufacturing, procurement, quality, or service systems.

FIVE IDENTITY DIMENSIONS

ROBOPARTS distinguishes five related identity dimensions.

A. PRODUCT IDENTITY

Identifies the manufacturer's product, product family, model, and commercial designation.

B. PHYSICAL IDENTITY

Identifies an individual manufactured physical component, including applicable serial number, production lot, and revision.

C. INSTALLATION IDENTITY

Connects the component to a specific robot, assembly, subsystem, joint, axis, or BOM position.

D. PERFORMANCE IDENTITY

Connects engineering specifications, test records, calibration, operating characteristics, and verified performance.

E. LIFECYCLE AND ACCOUNTABILITY IDENTITY

Connects manufacturing, custody, installation, service, maintenance, recall, replacement, and retirement information.

These dimensions must remain distinguishable while maintaining their relationships.

COMPONENT IDENTITY RELATIONSHIPS

The proposed manufacturer-to-physical-asset relationship is:

NAMESPACE | v COMPONENT | v MANUFACTURER | v PRODUCT | v MANUFACTURER PART NUMBER | v SKU | v REVISION | v LOT / SERIAL | v PHYSICAL ASSET | v BOM POSITION | v INSTALLATION | v SERVICE / LIFECYCLE | v DIGITAL IDENTITY

The architecture must distinguish a product model from an individual physical unit.

A product model may represent thousands of manufactured units.

Each serialized physical unit may have its own persistent identity and lifecycle record.

A replacement component must receive its own identity rather than inheriting the identity of the removed component.

==================================================

ROBOT-TO-COMPONENT HIERARCHY

A robotic system is an interconnected collection of physical assemblies and components.

The relationship can be represented as:

ROBOT SYSTEM | v ROBOT MODEL | v ROBOT INSTANCE | v SUBSYSTEM | v ASSEMBLY | v JOINT | v AXIS | v COMPONENT | v PHYSICAL PART

Example:

HUMANOID ROBOT | v RIGHT ARM | v SHOULDER ASSEMBLY | v SHOULDER JOINT | v ROTARY ACTUATOR | v MOTOR | v STATOR / ROTOR

Each level can maintain a separate identity while preserving its relationship to the larger robotic system.

WEB3 IDENTITY AND RESOLUTION

The Web3 namespace architecture is intended to provide persistent, resolvable identity references for physical components and associated records.

A namespace may be represented through a human-readable identity designation.

Illustrative example:

ST-RZ-004-0002-YT1P.endeffector

Illustrative actuator identity:

PITN-RB-000001.actuator

The identity format must support unique identification, namespace classification, and resolution to the associated component information.

The architecture may incorporate:

Decentralized identifiers. Verifiable credentials. Cryptographic signatures. Public identity references. Permission-controlled records. Digital passports. Tamper-evident event histories. Blockchain-anchored verification.

The implementation must distinguish public identity information from confidential manufacturer, commercial, operational, and personal information.

Sensitive technical data should not automatically be published on a public blockchain.

Blockchain anchoring may establish integrity and verification of records without requiring all underlying records to be stored on-chain.

A persistent identifier must not depend solely on a mutable product description, manufacturer URL, SKU, or database location.

MULTI-ROBOT INTEROPERABILITY

ROBOPARTS provides a conceptual information structure for relating components, capabilities, identities, and tasks across multiple robotic platforms.

The architecture can support relationships among:

Multiple robot models. Individual robot instances. Physical components. Robot configurations. Capabilities. Tasks. Operating environments. Lifecycle states. Digital representations.

Conceptually:

COMPONENTS | v CAPABILITIES | v ROBOT IDENTITIES | v TASK REQUIREMENTS | v ROBOT MATCHING | v APPLICATIONS

Actual multi-robot coordination requires compatible software, communication interfaces, authorization, and validated implementation.
